Why small tools win

8 November 2026 · 4 min read

  • tools
  • unix

There's a certain satisfaction in finding a tool that does exactly one thing and does it well. No dashboard, no account, no onboarding flow. You run it, it answers your question, and you get on with your day.

The cost of "just one more feature"

Most tools don't become complicated on purpose. They get there one reasonable request at a time. Each addition makes sense in isolation, but together they turn a focused utility into something that needs documentation to use.

The alternative is not always writing your own tool. Often it's finding the smaller existing one, or accepting a manual step that takes thirty seconds and requires no maintenance.

What I look for now

  • Predictable input and output. I should be able to guess what happens before I run it.
  • Composability. It should play nicely with pipes, files, and other tools.
  • Low ceremony. If I need to read a tutorial for a five-second task, something is wrong.
  • Boring storage. Plain text is a feature, not a limitation.
# A tool that fits the description
find . -name '*.log' -mtime +30 -print | xargs rm

Small is not the same as limited

A small tool can be deeply sophisticated internally. The point is that the sophistication is hidden behind a narrow interface. That's the same principle we use in software design: a good abstraction reduces the number of things you have to hold in your head.

The best tool for a job is often the one you can forget you're using.

I've started treating tool choices as a design decision rather than a preference. Every extra option is a small tax paid by every future user — including future me.
